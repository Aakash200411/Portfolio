Retail demand forecasting & inventory planning

A 28-day demand forecast on the M5 retail dataset, validated with rolling-origin backtests and turned into safety-stock and reorder-point scenarios.

Repository M5 dataset

Overview

I built an end-to-end forecasting pipeline on the M5 dataset: data validation, leakage-aware product selection, feature engineering on lagged and rolling demand, calendar and price covariates, and model comparison across four consecutive, non-overlapping 28-day validation windows. Each forecast origin only sees information available before its test period.

The forecast is then used for planning: lead-time demand, safety stock, reorder points and suggested order quantities, with a sensitivity analysis over lead time and service level.

The machine-learning model did not win. On sparse, noisy item-level demand, a simple statistical baseline beat tuned XGBoost, and the final forecast uses whichever model scored best in the backtests rather than assuming the more complex one should.

Method

  • Validation: checked duplicate identifiers, calendar and price key uniqueness, and consistency between the validation and evaluation series.
  • No leakage: products were selected using only the earliest training window, and all lag and rolling features use past observations only.
  • Metrics: MAE, RMSE, WAPE and forecast bias, reported per window with standard deviation so stability is visible, not just the average.
  • Tuning: three bounded XGBoost configurations compared with time-aware validation.
  • Inventory: 7-day lead time, 7-day review period and 95% service level as explicit assumptions, then re-run at other lead times and service levels.

Limits of the data

This is a focused demonstration, not a full M5 competition solution: one store (CA_1) and six selected item-store series, one of which lacked complete evaluation history and was excluded from the final forecast. The tuned XGBoost score is a model-selection result, not an untouched test score. Lead time, review period, service level and on-hand stock are scenario assumptions, so the order quantities are illustrative rather than purchasing advice.

What I'd add next

  • Scale to all stores and a hierarchical reconciliation of item, category and store forecasts.
  • Probabilistic forecasts (quantile models) so safety stock comes from the forecast distribution rather than a normal approximation.
  • Intermittent-demand methods such as Croston/TSB for the sparse series.
